# Life After Listing

Episode ID: RCF-06-LIFE-AFTER-LISTING · Series: ROAD TO THE EXCHANGE · 4/4 · Duration: 198.1 s

## Narration script (as recorded)

**[1] The Road to the Stock Exchange · Part 4 – Life after listing** (0.4s)

Listing day gets the headlines. But the real commitment starts the day after. Here's what life looks like as a listed company in Zimbabwe. This is Reian Creative Finance. Understanding markets. Following capital.

**[2] New owners – Shareholders and their rights** (14.7s)

First, you have new owners. Ordinary shares usually carry votes at general meetings, where shareholders elect directors and approve major decisions. Dividends are paid only when the board declares them out of profits. Nothing is guaranteed.

_On-screen source note: General principles; exact rights are set by the company's constitution and the Companies Act._

**[3] The reporting calendar – Deadlines that never stop** (29.9s)

Then the reporting calendar. On both the Z S E and the V F E X, interim results are due within three months of the half year end. Audited annual results must be published within three months of year end, and the annual report sent to shareholders within six months, and at least 21 days before the annual general meeting. And price sensitive news must be disclosed promptly.

_On-screen source note: Sources: ZSE SI 134/2019 ss.35, 36, 39; VFEX SI 62/2026 rr.37, 40 (same deadlines)._

**[4] Price and value – Share price and market capitalisation** (52.5s)

Now your share price and market capitalisation. Market cap is simply shares in issue times the share price, and it changes every trading day. Our example company from part three has 40 million shares. At 50 cents it's worth 20 million dollars. At 60 cents, 24 million. A weak share price can make it harder to raise money later.

_On-screen source note: Illustration uses the hypothetical company from Part 3._

**[5] Corporate governance – Running a listed company properly** (74.4s)

Governance matters even more after listing. Investors expect a board that challenges management, including independent directors, audit and remuneration committees, clear rules on conflicts of interest and related party deals, and an annual general meeting where shareholders can question the board face to face. Remember Nvidia from our global markets episode: when governance fails, the share price feels it.

_On-screen source note: General good practice expected of listed companies; specific requirements are set by the exchange rules and the Companies Act._

**[6] Using the listing – What a listing lets you do** (99.1s)

A listing also opens doors. A company can do a rights issue, offering new shares to existing shareholders first. It can use its shares to pay for acquisitions, reward staff through employee share schemes, and it gains visibility with lenders and partners.

_On-screen source note: General corporate-finance uses; each requires the relevant approvals._

**[7] The trade-off – Advantages vs disadvantages** (115.8s)

So it's a trade off. On one side: access to capital, liquidity for owners, credibility and shares you can use as a currency. On the other: ongoing compliance costs, public scrutiny of every result, some loss of control, and pressure from the market every single day.

_On-screen source note: General comparison. Fee and cost levels vary; the ZSE's 2019 fee schedule may have changed._

**[8] Oversight – Who is watching** (133.5s)

And you're always being watched. The exchange enforces its rules, the Securities and Exchange Commission oversees the market, and the Reserve Bank's foreign exchange rules cover foreign shareholdings and any listing abroad, which needs its prior approval. Serious breaches can lead to your shares being suspended.

_On-screen source note: Sources: SI 134/2019; SI 62/2026; RBZ FX Guidelines March 2026 ss.4.2.8, 4.2.18._

**[9] The exit doors – Suspension and delisting** (153.1s)

Finally, the exit doors. A suspension halts trading, often because results are late or information is unclear. Delisting means the company leaves the exchange, either voluntarily, for example to move to another exchange, or because it no longer meets the rules. TSL, for example, was reported to end its Z S E listing in June 2026 and move to the V F E X.

_On-screen source note: Example: The Herald (pre-event report). Process details depend on the exchange's rules._

**[10] Series takeaway – Listing is a marriage, not a wedding** (177.6s)

Listing is a marriage, not a wedding. Prepare for years, report on time every time, and treat your shareholders as partners. That's the end of our road to the stock exchange series. This is educational content, not legal or investment advice. Follow Reian Creative Finance for more.
