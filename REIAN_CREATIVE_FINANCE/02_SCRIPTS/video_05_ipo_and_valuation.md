# IPO, Valuation and Raising Capital

Episode ID: RCF-05-IPO-VALUATION · Series: ROAD TO THE EXCHANGE · 3/4 · Duration: 202.0 s

## Narration script (as recorded)

**[1] The Road to the Stock Exchange · Part 3 – IPO, valuation and raising capital** (0.4s)

How does a company raise millions of dollars on the stock exchange, and what happens to the owners' stake when it does? Let's work through it with real numbers. This is Reian Creative Finance. Understanding markets. Following capital.

**[2] Three ways onto the board – IPO, introduction, placement** (14.9s)

There are three main ways onto an exchange. An initial public offering, or I P O, offers shares to the public and can raise new money. News reports say Amalgamated Brands is raising about 20 million dollars this way on the V F E X. A listing by introduction raises no money: shares that are already widely held simply start trading. TSL was reported to move to the V F E X this way in 2026. And a private placement sells shares to selected investors.

_On-screen source note: Examples from news reports (Dealroom, Rio Times, The Herald). Not recommendations._

**[3] Where does the money go? – New shares vs offer for sale** (48.0s)

Now a crucial difference. When a company issues new shares, the cash goes to the company, and existing owners are diluted. In an offer for sale, existing owners sell their own shares: the cash goes to them, and the number of shares doesn't change. Many I P Os mix both. Amalgamated Brands' offer was reported to include 5 million dollars of new shares within the 20 million total.

_On-screen source note: Amalgamated Brands' offer was reported to include US$5m of new shares within the US$20m total._

**[4] Worked example · hypothetical company – Zim Agro Ltd raises US$5m** (71.7s)

Let's work an example with a made up company, Zim Agro Limited. It's valued at 15 million US dollars before the raise. That's the pre money valuation. It has 30 million shares, so each share is priced at 15 million divided by 30 million: 50 US cents. It wants to raise 5 million dollars of new money, so it issues 5 million divided by 50 cents: 10 million new shares.

_On-screen source note: Hypothetical company and numbers, for illustration only._

**[5] Worked example · after the IPO – What changes** (95.2s)

After the I P O there are 40 million shares. At 50 cents, the post money valuation, which is also the market capitalisation, is 20 million dollars. New investors own 10 million out of 40 million: 25 percent. The founders drop from 100 percent to 75 percent. That's dilution. But their stake is still worth 15 million dollars, and the company now has 5 million dollars of fresh cash to grow.

_On-screen source note: Hypothetical. Market capitalisation = shares in issue × share price, and moves every day once trading starts._

**[6] Worked example · the float test – Is 25% enough for the ZSE?** (121.0s)

But wait. The Z S E main board wants 30 percent of shares in public hands, and our public only has 25 percent. One fix: the founders also sell 2 million of their existing shares in an offer for sale. That money, 1 million dollars, goes to the founders, not the company. Now the public holds 12 million out of 40 million: exactly 30 percent.

_On-screen source note: Float rule: SI 134/2019 s.87 (30%; 20% offered if there was a prior private placement). Hypothetical numbers. 'Subscribed capital' (s.87) is a balance-sheet test, not market value._

**[7] The process – From pricing to trading** (143.8s)

The process runs like this. Advisers value the company, using its earnings, its assets and comparable listed companies. The prospectus is prepared and approved, and the offer opens. Investors apply, and if the offer is oversubscribed, allocations get scaled back. Finally the exchange grants the listing, and the shares start trading.

_On-screen source note: General sequence; exact approvals per COBE Act prospectus provisions (ss.104–114), SECZ and the exchange's rules._

**[8] Using the money – What companies do with IPO cash** (165.3s)

And what happens to the money? Companies use it to expand capacity, pay down expensive debt, or buy other businesses. As an investor, always read the use of proceeds section in the prospectus: it tells you whether your money is building the business, or just buying out the sellers.

_On-screen source note: General practice. The prospectus states the intended use of proceeds._

**[9] Part 3 takeaway – Dilution isn't loss** (182.9s)

Dilution isn't loss: it can mean a smaller slice of a bigger pie. Know who gets the cash, and check the public float rule. In part four: life after listing. This is educational content, not legal or investment advice. Follow Reian Creative Finance for more.
